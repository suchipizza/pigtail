The 30-second version

Pigtail's synthesis of the evidence below. Evidence level: Strong inference.

Plausible is an open source, cookie-free web analytics tool that a solo developer started in December 2018 and later ran with a marketing co-founder. The company says it has never paid for ads, affiliates or sponsorships. Its growth has come mainly from opinionated blog posts that reached the Hacker News front page, comparison and educational content that ranks in Google, and word of mouth. Revenue comes only from subscriptions to the hosted version, priced by pageviews with a 30-day free trial. It reached $1M ARR in June 2022, and later reported more than 21,000 paying subscribers and over 29,000 GitHub stars.

Growth mechanism

The claims point to a loop in which content and open source feed each other. Marko's posts against Google Analytics, and his posts about open source, repeatedly reached the Hacker News front page. Those posts drove traffic and trials, and spilled over to GitHub, where Plausible appeared on the Trending list. Open source code and aligned values led people to write about and recommend Plausible. Educational and comparison posts built steady Google search traffic, which became the top source of trial signups. Paying customers then spread the word. Several GitHub star spikes happened around the same days as Plausible-related Hacker News posts, for example April 8, 2020, July 27, 2020, October 6–13, 2020 and March 7, 2021. That overlap is timing only.

Origin and first users

Why it was built and how the earliest users were found, as far as public sources show.
Origin

Uku Täht began building Plausible around December 2018. While working on a startup, he was asked to install Google Analytics. He did not trust it, and he found existing alternatives such as Simple Analytics and Fathom too barebones. He set out to build a simpler Google Analytics alternative with better privacy, offering '80% of the benefit with 20% of the complexity'. He planned never to store IP addresses or sell data, and considered open-sourcing the code so people could verify those claims.

First users

Before writing much code, Uku published a blog post describing the tool he wanted and recruited beta users through Twitter and email. About 20–30 people showed interest. The beta was announced on Twitter and Indie Hackers, not through a big launch; sources place it in January or February 2019. All early users came from these build-in-public updates, and early users were mostly developers and personal bloggers. Paid plans opened in May 2019 to about 60 beta users, who got a third off. That month ended at $64 MRR.

Reusable tactics

Concrete actions another team could understand and adapt, with where and when this project used them.

Opinionated anti-incumbent essay seeded to developer communities

Write a long post arguing against the dominant tool and share it on Hacker News, Reddit, Lobsters, Indie Hackers, Twitter and Mastodon.

Mechanism: It reaches audiences already skeptical of the incumbent, and front-page placement brings concentrated traffic and trials.

April 8, 2020: Published 'Why you should stop using Google Analytics on your website' (~2,500 words) and shared it widely. It reached the top of Hacker News. Reported first-day visitors vary by source: 25,000+, about 30,000, and 50,000+ over a few days.

July 27, 2020: Published an article on paying rent with an open source project, which hit the Hacker News front page.

Needs: A clear position against a widely used incumbent

Rewrite the homepage headline to name the incumbent and compare against it directly.

Mechanism: Visitors immediately understand what the product replaces, which suits searchers already looking for an alternative.

How the strategy changed

Growth approach over time (strategy phases), shown separately from the business's situation (company stages).
Strategy phases
  1. later yearsOpen core and conversion refinement

    Plausible kept premium features cloud-only, renamed the self-hosted release to Community Edition and introduced a Business plan. In 2026 it simplified its homepage to improve trial conversion.

  2. 2018 – early 2020Build in public and niche communities

    Uku shared progress on a blog, Twitter and Indie Hackers. All early users came from these build-in-public updates. For about a year, blog posts, outreach and HN posts brought only minor success.

  3. Mar 2020 – 2021Content marketing and positioning against Google Analytics

    After Marko joined, Plausible repositioned itself as an explicit Google Analytics alternative. It published blog posts weekly or more often. Several posts reached the Hacker News front page, and Google search became the top source of trial signups.

  4. Jun – Oct 2020Open-source self-hosting and copyleft relicensing

    Plausible released a Docker self-hosted beta in June 2020 and v1 in October 2020. In October 2020 it moved from MIT to AGPL to stop corporations reselling its code. Both announcements topped Hacker News.

  5. 2022 – 2023Capturing regulatory and incumbent-change demand

    Plausible saw increased interest after the Austrian DPA ruling against Google Analytics. It shipped a Google Analytics import tool, and launched funnels and revenue goals timed to the Universal Analytics sunset.

Company stages
  1. Dec 2018 – May 2019Solo-founder side project, free beta

    Uku Täht started Plausible alone in December 2018 as a simpler, privacy-friendly Google Analytics alternative. He ran a free beta with no subscriptions for about five months. Sources disagree on the beta date: one says January 2019 on Indie Hackers, another says February.

  2. May 2019 – Mar 2020Solo, paid but low MRR

    Paid subscriptions launched in May 2019 with 60 beta users, ending that month at $64 MRR. It took 324 days to reach $400 MRR. Plausible's monthly recaps report that revenue was flat in January 2020 because of higher-than-normal cancellations, and that about 25% of revenue went to transaction fees. Sources disagree on the customer count when co-founder Marko Saric joined in 2020: about 100, a couple hundred, or a few hundred.

  3. 2021 – mid 2022Profitable small team scaling ARR

    Plausible was a profitable two-person team in 2021 and declined VC approaches. It reached $500k ARR ($42,624 MRR) in October 2021 and $1M ARR on 2 June 2022 with 7,000+ subscribers. The team grew to four.

  4. 2022 – presentEstablished independent, profitable company

    Plausible remains self-funded and profitable, with a team of 8 to 10. Reported subscriber counts rose over time: 12,000+, then more than 15,000, then more than 21,000. It says it multiplied its $1M ARR several times in the following three years. It has turned down investment offers.

What Plausible did differently

Higher-level interpretation, separate from the factual record above.

Several choices stand out. Plausible positioned itself directly as 'the alternative to Google Analytics', which the company says started its growth. It made cookie-free tracking the default, so customers do not need consent banners. It refused paid advertising, affiliate programs and sales calls, even when some publishers declined to list it for having no affiliate program. It published traffic and revenue numbers openly. It relicensed from MIT to AGPL in 2020 after larger companies sought to resell its self-hosted version, and later moved some premium features to the cloud-only offering. Whether these choices drove growth more than broader privacy trends is not settled; Marko Saric also credits the general privacy trend.

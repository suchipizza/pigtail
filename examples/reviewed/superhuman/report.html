The 30-second version

Pigtail's synthesis of the evidence below. Evidence level: Summarized from cited sources.

Superhuman began as a paid-only email client and launched invite-only in 2017. Every user paid about $30 a month, and new users were onboarded one-to-one on a live call. Growth came mainly through referrals that let contacts skip the waitlist, a 'Sent via Superhuman' email footer, and evergreen content, all guided by a 'very disappointed' product-market fit survey. It later moved toward teams and enterprise. Grammarly acquired it in 2025 and then renamed itself Superhuman, folding the email app into a multi-product suite as Superhuman Mail.

Growth mechanism

The early loop appears to have worked like this. Concierge onboarding created committed users, and the company says these users drove word of mouth. Users could refer waitlisted contacts so they skipped the line, and one interview put weekly virally referred new users at 70%. The footer on every sent email reportedly drove 50–60% of web traffic. Later, thread sharing let non-users comment inline and gave them conversion paths into the product. Superhuman reports that launching this feature produced its highest weeks of expansion revenue. It also reports that teams using collaborative features grow seats and revenue twice as fast as other teams.

Origin and first users

Why it was built and how the earliest users were found, as far as public sources show.
Origin

The founder founded Rapportive, a Gmail plug-in, and sold it to LinkedIn in 2012. He worked there for about two years, then sketched the concept and incorporated Superhuman in February 2014. He says he conceived it because Gmail and its plugins were getting slower and more cluttered. The idea was a fast, keyboard-driven, offline-capable email client, pitched as 'the fastest email experience ever made.' He raised a seed round of roughly $750K–$800K, mostly from earlier backers, and bought superhuman.com for $175,000, paid over seven years. From the start he planned virality, including email signatures, as a core channel. Design began in January 2015, and the first code was written on May 4, 2015.

First users

A landing page went live around May or June 2015 and drew 10–30 signups a day. Each signup got an automated email from the founder asking what email tool they used and what they hated about it. Sources put the number of first-year interviews at about 700 or about 1,000. A Medium post about surviving an acquisition, written after the Mailbox shutdown, reportedly brought more than 5,000 signups. Early targets were founders, VCs, executives and managers at high-growth tech companies. The founder personally onboarded the first 100–200 or so customers in long sessions. According to the company, no early user got the product free; even investors paid.

Reusable tactics

Concrete actions another team could understand and adapt, with where and when this project used them.

'Very disappointed' PMF survey as the product OKR

Email active users a short survey asking how they would feel if they could no longer use the product. Segment the responses to the most enthusiastic personas. Make the 'very disappointed' percentage the only key result for the product team.

Mechanism: It turns product-market fit into a tracked number. That number guides roadmap choices: what to double down on for fans and what is holding back users who would only be 'somewhat disappointed'.

summer 2017: Sent the survey via Typeform by email, segmented to founders, managers, executives and business development roles, and built tooling to survey new users continuously.

Needs: A base of active users to poll (Superhuman had 100–200 users at the first survey
Documented

Human concierge onboarding

Onboard every new user in a live one-to-one call, and fix bugs found in onboarding before the next weekly cohort.

Mechanism: Founder-attributed: onboarded users showed strong retention, low churn, high virality and high NPS, and the calls created superfans who drove word of mouth.

2017 – c. 2023: The founder personally onboarded the early customers; sources variously say the first 100, 200 or several hundred. The work then scaled to onboarding specialists, with sources citing 14 and up to 20 full-time people at peak.

Documented

Metered weekly onboarding instead of a public launch

Choose a weekly number of new users to admit from a waitlist rather than opening access all at once.

Mechanism: It keeps onboarding quality high and lets the team fix issues between cohorts. The founder says the purpose was to keep users happy, not to create scarcity.

c. 2017–2019: Superhuman picked a weekly onboarding number and ran this way for two years. One source says growth was capped at 100 new users per week.

Documented

Skip-the-waitlist referrals

Let existing users refer waitlisted contacts so those contacts get access sooner, using a non-monetary incentive.

Mechanism: Faster access is a strong incentive when demand exceeds supply, so referrals become the fastest path in.

Date unknown: Users could refer waitlisted contacts to skip the line. Users also gave out invites publicly, for example in Twitter threads.

Needs: A waitlist
Documented

Branded 'Sent via' email footer

Add a product-branded footer to outgoing emails so that every email sent advertises the product.

Mechanism: Each sent email exposes recipients to the brand; this was planned as a virality channel from the start.

Date unknown: Superhuman added a 'Sent via Superhuman' footer. A secondary source reports it contributed 50–60% of web traffic.

Documented

Van Westendorp premium pricing

Ask early users at what price the product feels expensive but still worth buying, and set a premium price from the median answer.

Mechanism: It anchors the price to what users will tolerate and supports premium positioning against free incumbents.

late 2015: Surveyed about 100 early customers. Sources give the median answer as $29 or about $30; the price was set at $29 and then rounded to $30.

Needs: Early paying users to survey (about 100)
Documented

Automated founder discovery email to every signup

Auto-send each landing-page signup a short email from the founder asking what they use and what they hate about it.

Mechanism: It turns signups into qualitative research and interview leads at scale.

2015: A Squarespace landing page auto-sent two survey questions, leading to hundreds of customer interviews in the first year.

Needs: A landing page collecting emails
Documented

Open-sourcing an internal framework as evergreen content

Publish the company's internal operating method as a widely shareable article.

Mechanism: Evergreen content keeps driving organic traffic and leads long after it is published.

2018: The founder published the product-market fit engine on First Round Review. It became that outlet's most widely shared entrepreneurship article of the year.

Documented

Non-user collaboration surface with conversion paths

Let non-users view and comment on shared content, using notification emails and paths to sign up.

Mechanism: Collaboration pulls colleagues into the product, creating viral and expansion loops.

Date unknown: Built email thread sharing with inline comments for anyone, with notifications and invites layered on.

Needs: Multiplayer features in the core product

Two-stage product-led sales

Staff sales first on 'hand raiser' leads, then use automated outreach for weaker usage signals.

Mechanism: It focuses human sales effort on the highest-intent accounts and covers the long tail with automation.

Date unknown: Superhuman applied this as part of moving up-market with team purchasing.

How the strategy changed

Growth approach over time (strategy phases), shown separately from the business's situation (company stages).
Strategy phases
  1. growth stage (c. 2021–2025)Multiplayer features and product-led sales to teams

    Superhuman adapted its existing product for teams. It shipped multiplayer features such as shared snippets and thread sharing, added viral loops on top of them, and ran product-led sales. It also built sales-specific features and integrations.

  2. 2015 – 2016Landing-page-led customer discovery

    A landing page collected signups, and each signup got an automated email asking about their email tool and frustrations. This led to hundreds of founder interviews in the first year: about 700 or about 1,000, depending on the source.

  3. 2017 – c. 2023Concierge onboarding, waitlist and referral-driven growth

    New users were onboarded one-to-one at a chosen weekly pace instead of through a traditional launch. Referrals let people skip the waitlist. Growth relied on PR, content and organic word of mouth.

  4. 2017 – 2018Product-market-fit engine before launch

    From April 2017, Superhuman measured product-market fit with the 'very disappointed' survey and segmented to its target personas. It split the roadmap between strengthening what fans loved and closing gaps such as mobile. The launch was held back until the score improved.

  5. Oct 2025 – presentSuite bundling, acquisitions and AI agents

    After the rebrand, the company bundled its products into a suite, launched Superhuman Go with partner agents, and opened a partner/reseller program. It also acquired or agreed to acquire GPTZero, Fathom and Rows.

Company stages
  1. Feb 2014 – 2017Pre-launch, seed-funded build

    The founder incorporated Superhuman in 2014 after leaving LinkedIn and raised a seed round of roughly $750k–$800k; the amount differs between sources. The first version was desktop/Chrome and Gmail only and took most of 18 months to build. Two to three years in, there were still no customers and few outside users, and the team chose not to launch publicly.

  2. 2017 – Aug 2021Invite-only, paid-only, venture-backed growth

    Superhuman launched invite-only in 2017 with no freemium tier; every user paid $30/month. It raised a $33M Series B in 2019 and a $75M Series C in August 2021. Customer counts stayed in the tens of thousands while the waitlist grew to hundreds of thousands.

  3. c. 2021 – mid-2025Growth stage moving up-market to teams and enterprise

    With just over 100 employees, Superhuman shifted from prosumers to teams, enterprise and sales users. It added Outlook and CRM integrations and landed larger enterprise deals, including a 2,500-seat consulting firm deal in mid-2024.

  4. July 2025 – presentAcquired; part of the multi-product Superhuman suite (formerly Grammarly)

    Grammarly announced it would acquire Superhuman in July 2025, and the deal closed in October 2025. Grammarly then renamed itself Superhuman and became a multi-product company spanning Grammarly, Coda/Docs, Mail and Go, sold as a bundled subscription. The combined company reports more than 40M daily users and more than $700M in annual revenue.

What Superhuman did differently

Higher-level interpretation, separate from the factual record above.

Superhuman stands out for withholding a public launch until a measurable product-market fit signal improved, rather than launching early. The founder has described resisting team pressure to launch. According to the founder, the 'very disappointed' share was made the sole key result of a product OKR. Superhuman also used human onboarding as its go-to-market for a $30-a-month product. It priced at the premium end using a Van Westendorp survey and offered no freemium tier, in contrast to free incumbents.
